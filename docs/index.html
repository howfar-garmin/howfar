A free data field for Garmin watches

How many times have you wanted to know how long to the next checkpoint?

You asked a volunteer, then got angry at how far off they were. HowFar puts the answer on your wrist: the time to the next checkpoint of your course. It learns how you climb and descend while you run, with nothing to set up, and improves the answer every second.

On your watch

What the field shows

Add HowFar to one of your activity's data screens, load a course with checkpoints, and run. It shows three lines; a full-screen field shows four, with the arrival time beside the number.

P Planaval
1:55h@16:40
via Col de la Crosatie in 0:45
then Valgrisenche @ 18:05
  1. Top line: the next main checkpoint. The name comes from your course, or from a point you added on the race prep page, such as the top of a climb or a hut. The letter in front tells you how much the watch knows about the trail ahead: P it knows every climb and descent to the checkpoint (you gave it the course profile, see Prepare a race); ~ it only knows how far away and how much higher or lower the checkpoint is.
  2. Big number: time to get there while you keep moving, only as precisely as it can be known: hours and minutes in 30-minute steps from four hours out, 15-minute steps from two and 5-minute steps from one (2:15 h), whole minutes closer in (42 min), minutes and seconds in the last 5 minutes. For the first 4 km or so the number is grey and (learning) appears after the arrival time, or under the number on a full-screen field: it's only a rough guess, because HowFar is still learning how you're moving today. When it turns white, it has seen enough of your running. Time spent stopped at an aid station is not included, because nobody can guess how long you'll stay.
  3. Arrival time. The time of day you'll get there, in your watch's 12 or 24 hour format: on the line below the number, or beside it on a full-screen field.
  4. On a full-screen field, only with the course profile: two more lines. In grey, the next minor point on the way, such as a climb top or a hut you added (via Col de la Crosatie in 0:45); below it, the main checkpoint after the next one (then Valgrisenche @ 18:05). With no minor point ahead, the two lines show the next two main checkpoints instead: the first with the time to go (then La Thuile in 1:35), the second with its arrival time. You choose which points are main and which minor on the race prep page. Your watch tells a data field only about the next checkpoint, so without the profile these lines stay empty.

Without a course, or between courses, the field shows --. On a screen with many fields it shows just the big number.

How it works

It learns how you move in the mountains

Nothing to set up, nothing to paste: HowFar learns your pace while you run, and keeps learning all race long.

Your pace on every slope

Every 25 m it notes how steep the trail is and how fast you're going, from steep descents to steep climbs. Slopes you haven't met yet today it fills in from the nearest ones you have, the way pace usually changes with steepness. It needs no practice run: on your first race it adapts within the first few kilometres. What it remembers from earlier runs helps a little later in the race, and counts less and less as today's kilometres add up.

The trail ahead

If you give it the course profile, it knows every climb and descent to the checkpoint and adds up your pace on each. Without it, it only knows the total height difference, which is rougher on up-and-down trails.

How your day is going

It watches your last couple of kilometres: if you're slowing down, or picking up again, the time ahead follows. It also corrects for your watch measuring more distance than the course map: switchbacks and GPS wobble add about 15% on the Tor.

Does it work?

Tested on six real races

HowFar works best where it was made and tested: trail runs of 25 km or more with real climbs and descents. Here, six races from 26 to 337 km; behind them, 129 training runs of at least 25 km.

Each race was replayed and the prediction checked against reality at every moment, as if it were the runner's first race with HowFar: nothing remembered from earlier runs. “Average pace so far” is the usual estimate, the distance left at your average moving speed since the start. Lower minutes and a higher “less than 5 min off” are better.
  • Race columns and Average: how many minutes off the watch was, on average, whenever the next checkpoint was less than an hour away.
  • Less than 5 or 15 min off: out of every 100 times the watch showed a time, how many were less than 5 (or 15) minutes off.
  • Off by, 2 km before: how far off the watch was when you were 2 km from each checkpoint, the moment you often check whether to eat or drink now.
  • STL25's route had no checkpoints, so it was split into two stretches of 13 km.
  • Abbots Way: the race's file has no checkpoints, so they were put where the runner stopped (mostly aid stations), with one more every 20 km where stops were far apart.
  • Tor des Géants: the 2023 race replayed on the official 2026 course; the few legs where the route has changed since are left out of the score.
  • TORX100: the 2025 race replayed on the official 2026 course, which it matches all the way. Almost every checkpoint is a col or a hut, so each leg is one climb or one descent: knowing the height difference is nearly enough, and the course profile adds little.
What else was tried

The same races were used to test other ways of predicting: classic hiking rules (Naismith, Tobler), ITRA's km-effort, an energy-cost model of running uphill and downhill, a machine-learning model, a correction for slowing down with distance, carrying today's trend forward, a correction every few kilometres based on the last stretch, and a pace made from your past races and pasted before the start. None did better, so HowFar doesn't use them. The pasted pace helped in the first hours of races like the ones it was made from, and hurt when they were different; learning on the run catches up within a few kilometres anyway.

  • Minetti A.E. et al. (2002), Energy cost of walking and running at extreme uphill and downhill slopes.
  • Naismith's rule, and Tobler's hiking function in a crowd-sourced revalidation.
  • ITRA, km-effort.
  • Pacing decline in a 173 km trail race, PeerJ 2016.
  • Real-time performance prediction in long-distance trail running, Sports 2025.

Install

Put it on your watch

HowFar is free in the Garmin Connect IQ Store, for the Enduro 3, fēnix 8 and epix Pro (Gen 2). You install it from your phone; nothing to copy, no computer needed.

1. Install it from the store

  1. On your phone, open the Connect IQ Store: in the Garmin Connect app it's in your watch's menu, or use the separate Connect IQ app.
  2. Search for HowFar and open it.
  3. Tap Install, and pick your watch if it asks.
  4. Sync your watch with the phone. HowFar arrives on the watch at the next sync.

2. Add it to a data screen

  1. On the watch, open the activity you race with, for example Trail Run, but don't start it.
  2. Hold the MENU button (middle left) and open the activity's settings, then Data Screens.
  3. Pick a screen, choose the field to replace, then Connect IQ → HowFar.
  4. Tip: give it a screen with one or two fields, so the checkpoint name and arrival time fit. On a screen of its own it also shows the checkpoint after the next.

3. Race with a course

  1. Load the race course on the watch as usual (Navigate → Courses), ideally one with the checkpoints marked.
  2. Start the activity. HowFar shows the next checkpoint and counts down.
  3. For the first 4 km or so the time is grey, with (learning) after the arrival time: a rough guess while HowFar learns how you're moving today. Then it turns white. No practice run is needed beforehand.
  4. Save the activity at the end: HowFar remembers what it learned for your next race.
No phone at hand? Copy it with a cable instead
  1. Download the file for your watch model from the latest release on GitHub, for example HowFar-enduro3.prg for an Enduro 3.
  2. Connect the watch to your computer with its charging cable.
  3. Windows: open File Explorer; the watch appears like a phone. Mac: install the free app OpenMTP and open it (close Garmin Express first if it's running).
  4. Open the folder GARMIN, then Apps, and drag the downloaded file into it. Unplug the watch: after a few seconds HowFar is installed.

Good to know: copied this way, HowFar works fully, but it has no settings, so you can't paste the course profile. To switch to the store version later, delete the file from GARMIN → Apps first, so you don't end up with two copies.

Prepare a race (optional)

Show it the trail ahead

HowFar works without any preparation: it learns your pace as you run. One optional extra makes it better: the course profile, pasted into the field's settings in the Garmin Connect app on your phone. Only with it does HowFar:

  • know every climb and descent to the checkpoint, not just how much higher or lower it is (P on the watch instead of ~);
  • show the checkpoint after the next and when you'll get there, on a full-screen field: your watch tells a data field only about the next one;
  • show points you add on the race prep page: the top and bottom of every big climb, huts and villages, or your own. Make each one main, to count down to it, or minor, to see it on the way. Your watch's course stays as it is.

This needs HowFar installed from the Connect IQ Store: if you copied the file onto the watch with a cable instead, Garmin Connect shows no settings for it.

Questions


Good to know






Does it need a phone or a signal during the race?
No. Everything happens on the watch. Your phone is only needed before the race, to paste the optional course profile.


Does it use more battery?
It uses no sensors of its own: it reads the distance, altitude and course information your watch already records, and does a little arithmetic once a second. The watch's GPS and screen use far more.


What about time at aid stations?
HowFar only counts the time you'll spend moving. It can't know whether you'll stop for two minutes or twenty, so stops are never included: not in the countdown, and not in the arrival time. If you plan to stop on the way, add that time in your head: with 1:10 h on the watch and a 15-minute break at an aid station before the checkpoint, expect to get there in about 1:25 h. While you're stopped the countdown waits, and when you set off again it carries on from where it was.


What if I go off course?
HowFar uses the distance to the next checkpoint that your watch's navigation gives it. While you're off the course line the estimate is rougher; once you're back on it, it settles again.


What if my watch's course has checkpoints the course profile doesn't?
No problem. HowFar works out where each checkpoint is on the profile from how far the finish is, so it still knows every climb and descent to it. It also copes with checkpoints the profile has and the watch doesn't, and with a course file that is a slightly different version of the race.


What if I slow down, or speed up, during the race?
It follows you. It keeps comparing how you're moving on each slope with what it expected over the last couple of kilometres. If you're, say, 10% slower than that, it assumes you'll keep that pace on everything ahead, and the time to the next checkpoint gets about 10% longer. When you pick up again, it follows you back within a kilometre or two. A single slow minute, like tying a shoe, doesn't change it.


Does it keep up as my fitness changes?
Yes. At the end of every activity with HowFar on a data screen, it adds what it learned to what it remembers, and older activities count a little less each time: after five more activities an old one counts less than half, after ten about a fifth. During a race, what it remembers counts less and less as today's kilometres add up, so today's running soon takes over.


Why is it off at the start of a race?
It's still learning how you're moving today. That's why, for the first 4 km or so, the time is grey and the arrival time ends with (learning): take it as a rough guess. When the time turns white, it has seen enough of your running.


Should I run with HowFar before a race?
No need. In tests, a first race with HowFar was as accurate as one where it remembered earlier runs: in the first half hour a little more, after that a little less. It adapts within the first few kilometres. What it remembers helps only a little, later in the race.


Can I give it my pace from past races?
Not any more. Early versions had a setting for it, and in tests it helped only in the first hours of races like the ones it was made from, and made things worse when they were different. Learning on the run catches up within a few kilometres, and HowFar already remembers your runs on its own.


Where does my data go?
Nowhere. The watch keeps what it learns, and the race files you use to prepare stay on your own computer or phone. The one exception is optional: on the race prep page, “Add huts and villages” asks OpenStreetMap what's around your course, sending only the corners of a rectangle around it.


Which watches does it work on?
Garmin Enduro 3, fēnix 8 (43 and 47 mm, Solar 47 and 51 mm) and epix Pro (Gen 2) 42, 47 and 51 mm. More models can be added.
