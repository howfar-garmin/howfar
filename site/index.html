A free data field for Garmin watches

How many times have you wanted to know how long to the next checkpoint?

You asked a volunteer, then got angry at how far off they were. HowFar puts the answer on your wrist: the time to the next checkpoint. It learns how you climb and descend as you run. There's nothing to set up, and it improves the answer every second.

On your watch

What the field shows

Add HowFar to a data screen, load a course with checkpoints, and run. It shows the next checkpoint, how long it will take to get there, and when you'll arrive. On a full screen, it also shows the points before and after it.

then Valgrisenche+1:25

  1. The next main checkpoint, with P if HowFar knows every climb and descent to it, or ~ if it only knows how much higher or lower it is.

    More

    The name comes from your course, or from a point you added on the race prep page, like the top of a climb or a hut. To get P, give HowFar the course profile (see Prepare a race).

  2. The time to get there if you keep moving. The dot shows how far off it may be.

    More

    The dot is green with less than 45 minutes to go: usually within 5 minutes. Yellow up to 2 hours: usually within 15. Red beyond that: often half an hour or more. The other times on a full screen have a dot too. The time is rounded to what can really be known: to 30 minutes beyond four hours, 15 minutes beyond two hours, 5 minutes beyond one hour (2:15 h), whole minutes closer in (42 min), and seconds in the last 5 minutes. For about the first 4 km the number is grey, with (learning) under it: it's only a rough guess while HowFar learns how you're moving today. When it turns white, it's ready. Stops at aid stations are not included, because nobody can guess how long you'll stay.

  3. The arrival time, in small print next to the number.

  4. On a full screen, with the course profile: the points around it, in the order you'll reach them. Minor points on the way are above the name. The next main checkpoints are below the number.

    More

    Minor points, like a climb top or a hut you added, are grey, with the time from now (Col de la Crosatie in 0:45). If there are too many to fit, the last line counts the rest (Lac du Fond in 1:05 (+2)). Each one you pass makes room for the next. For a main checkpoint, the time is for its leg only, from when you leave the checkpoint before it (then Valgrisenche +1:25). It leaves out your stop there, so add that yourself. You choose which points are main and which are minor on the race prep page. On a smaller field, like half the screen, HowFar shows only the countdown: the checkpoint, the time and the arrival. To have both, put HowFar on two data screens. Without the course profile these extra lines stay empty, because your watch only tells a data field about the next checkpoint.

With no course loaded, the field shows --. On a screen with many fields, it shows only the big number.

For each moment of the race: the time HowFar predicted (before rounding), minus the time it really took to reach the next checkpoint (stops not counted). What HowFar has learned today

How it works

It learns how you move in the mountains

Nothing to set up: HowFar learns your pace as you run, and keeps learning all race long.

Your pace on every slope

Every 25 m, it notes how steep the trail is and how fast you're going, on climbs and descents alike.

More

For slopes you haven't run yet today, it estimates from the nearest ones you have. It needs no practice run: in your first race it adapts within the first few kilometres. What it remembers from earlier runs helps a little later in the race, and counts less and less as the race goes on.

The trail ahead

With the course profile, it knows every climb and descent to the checkpoint, and adds up your pace on each one. Without it, it only knows the total height difference, which is less accurate on up-and-down trails.

How your day is going

It watches your last couple of kilometres. If you slow down or speed up, the time ahead changes with you.

More

It also allows for your watch measuring more distance than the map: switchbacks and GPS wobble add about 15% on the Tor.

Does it work?

Tested on six real races

HowFar works best on what it was made and tested for: trail runs of 25 km or more, with real climbs and descents. Here are six races, from 26 to 337 km. Behind them are 129 training runs of at least 25 km.

Each race was replayed, and the prediction was checked against what really happened at every moment. Each time, it was as if it were the runner's first race with HowFar, with nothing remembered from earlier runs. “Average pace so far” is the usual estimate: the distance left at your average moving speed since the start. Fewer minutes off, and a higher “less than 5 min off”, are better.
About these numbers
  • Race columns and Average: how many minutes HowFar was off, on average, whenever the next checkpoint was less than an hour away.
  • Less than 5 or 15 min off: out of every 100 times HowFar showed a time, how many were less than 5 (or 15) minutes off.
  • Off by, 2 km before: how far off HowFar was 2 km before each checkpoint, when you often decide whether to eat or drink now.
  • STL25 had no checkpoints on its route, so it was split into two 13 km parts.
  • Abbots Way: the race file has no checkpoints, so they were placed where the runner stopped (mostly aid stations), plus one every 20 km where stops were far apart.
  • Tor des Géants: the 2023 race replayed on the official 2026 course. The few legs where the route has changed are left out.
  • TORX100: the 2025 race replayed on the official 2026 course, which matches it all the way. Almost every checkpoint is a col or a hut, so each leg is a single climb or descent. Knowing the height difference is nearly enough, so the course profile adds little.
What else was tried

The same races were used to test other ways to predict: classic hiking rules (Naismith, Tobler), ITRA's km-effort, an energy-cost model of running uphill and downhill, a machine-learning model, a correction for slowing down over distance, carrying today's trend forward, a correction every few kilometres based on the last stretch, and a pace from your past races pasted before the start. None did better, so HowFar doesn't use them. The pasted pace helped in the first hours of races similar to the ones it came from, and hurt in different ones. Learning on the run catches up within a few kilometres anyway.

  • Minetti A.E. et al. (2002), Energy cost of walking and running at extreme uphill and downhill slopes.
  • Naismith's rule, and Tobler's hiking function in a crowd-sourced revalidation.
  • ITRA, km-effort.
  • Pacing decline in a 173 km trail race, PeerJ 2016.
  • Real-time performance prediction in long-distance trail running, Sports 2025.

Install

Put it on your watch

HowFar is free in the Garmin Connect IQ Store, for the Enduro 3, fēnix 8 and epix Pro (Gen 2). You install it from your phone; no computer needed.

1. Install it from the store

  1. On your phone, open the Connect IQ Store. It's in your watch's menu in the Garmin Connect app, or in the separate Connect IQ app.
  2. Search for HowFar and open it.
  3. Tap Install, and pick your watch if it asks.
  4. Sync the watch with your phone. After the sync, HowFar is on the watch.

2. Add it to a data screen

  1. On the watch, open the activity you race with, for example Trail Run. Don't start it yet.
  2. Hold MENU (the middle button on the left), open the activity's settings, then Data Screens.
  3. Pick a screen, choose the field to replace, then Connect IQ → HowFar.
  4. Tip: on a full data screen, HowFar lists the points ahead. On a screen with two fields, it shows only the countdown. You can have both, on two data screens.

3. Race with a course

  1. Load the race course on the watch as usual (Navigate → Courses), ideally one with the checkpoints marked.
  2. Start the activity. HowFar shows the next checkpoint and counts down.
  3. For about the first 4 km, the time is grey with (learning) under it: a rough guess while HowFar learns how you're moving today. Then it turns white. No practice run is needed.
  4. Save the activity at the end. HowFar remembers what it learned for your next race.
No phone at hand? Copy it with a cable
  1. Download the file for your watch from the latest release on GitHub, for example HowFar-enduro3.prg for an Enduro 3.
  2. Connect the watch to your computer with its charging cable.
  3. Windows: open File Explorer; the watch shows up like a phone. Mac: install the free app OpenMTP and open it (quit Garmin Express first if it's running).
  4. Open the GARMIN folder, then Apps, and drag the file into it. Unplug the watch: after a few seconds HowFar is installed.

Good to know: installed this way, HowFar works fully but has no settings, so you can't paste the course profile. To switch to the store version later, first delete the file from GARMIN → Apps, so you don't have two copies.

Prepare a race (optional)

Show it the trail ahead

HowFar works without any preparation: it learns your pace as you run. One optional extra makes it better: the course profile, pasted into HowFar's settings in the Garmin Connect app. Only with it can HowFar:

  • know every climb and descent to the checkpoint, not just how much higher or lower it is (P on the watch instead of ~);
  • show the points around the checkpoint on a full screen, with their times: the ones on the way and the next main ones. Your watch only tells a data field about the next checkpoint;
  • show points you add on the race prep page: tops and bottoms of big climbs, huts, villages, or your own. Make each one main, to count down to it, or minor, to see it on the way. The course on your watch doesn't change.

This needs HowFar from the Connect IQ Store. If you copied it onto the watch with a cable, Garmin Connect has no settings for it.

Open race prep Make it from your race's course file, right in your browser.

The course profile

It tells HowFar every climb and descent between checkpoints (the P on the watch). You make it from the race's course file, the same GPX you load on the watch. For a race in stages, like the Tor, make one per stage, and paste the next one at each life base.

Questions

        
Good to know

      

      

        
Does it need a phone or a signal during the race?
No. Everything happens on the watch. You only need your phone before the race, to paste the optional course profile.

        
Does it use more battery?
Hardly. It uses no sensors of its own: it reads the distance, altitude and course data your watch already records, and does a little math once a second. The GPS and the screen use far more.

        
What about time at aid stations?
HowFar only counts the time you'll spend moving. It can't know if you'll stop for two minutes or twenty, so stops are never included, neither in the countdown nor in the arrival time. If you plan to stop on the way, add that time yourself: with 1:10 h on the watch and a 15-minute stop before the checkpoint, expect to get there in about 1:25 h. The times of the next main checkpoints (then Valgrisenche +1:25) leave stops out too: each is the moving time for that leg, from when you leave the checkpoint before it. While you're stopped, the countdown pauses, and it carries on when you set off again.

        
What if I go off course?
HowFar uses the distance to the next checkpoint from your watch's navigation. While you're off the course, the estimate is rougher. Once you're back on it, it settles again.

        
What if my watch's course has checkpoints the course profile doesn't?
No problem. HowFar finds where each checkpoint is on the profile from its distance to the finish, so it still knows every climb and descent to it. It also copes with checkpoints the profile has and the watch doesn't, and with a course file that's a slightly different version of the race.

        
What if I slow down, or speed up, during the race?
It follows you. Over the last couple of kilometres, it compares how fast you're moving on each slope with what it expected. If you're, say, 10% slower, it assumes you'll stay that way, and the time to the next checkpoint gets about 10% longer. When you speed up again, it follows within a kilometre or two. One slow minute, like tying a shoe, doesn't change it.

        
Does it keep up as my fitness changes?
Yes. At the end of every activity with HowFar on a data screen, it adds what it learned to what it remembers. Older activities count a little less each time: after five more activities an old one counts less than half, after ten about a fifth. During a race, what it remembers counts less and less as you go, so today's running soon takes over.

        
Why is it off at the start of a race?
It's still learning how you're moving today. That's why, for about the first 4 km, the time is grey with (learning) under it: treat it as a rough guess. When the time turns white, it's ready.

        
Should I run with HowFar before a race?
No need. In tests, a first race with HowFar was about as accurate as one where it remembered earlier runs. It adapts within the first few kilometres. What it remembers helps only a little, later in the race.

        
Can I give it my pace from past races?
Not any more. Early versions had a setting for it. In tests it helped only in the first hours of races like the ones it came from, and made things worse in different ones. Learning on the run catches up within a few kilometres, and HowFar already remembers your runs on its own.

        
Where does my data go?
Nowhere. The watch keeps what it learns, and the race files you use stay on your own computer or phone. One optional exception: on the race prep page, “Add huts and villages” asks OpenStreetMap what's around your course. It sends only the corners of a rectangle around it.

        
Which watches does it work on?
Garmin Enduro 3, fēnix 8 (43 and 47 mm, Solar 47 and 51 mm) and epix Pro (Gen 2) 42, 47 and 51 mm. More models can be added.
